Plants,and rather a lot of them.

Succulents and cacti, mostly. What I grow, what I have learned, the resources I keep going back to — and the app I built when the spreadsheet gave up.

How this started

In September 2018, my ex-mother-in-law gave me a Graptoveria ‘Fred Ives.’ I kept it indoors in a tiny apartment with almost no natural light, and it got extremely leggy. That single question — why is this plant doing that? — opened a door I haven’t closed since.

I started researching. Succulents, I discovered, came in hundreds of shapes, colors, and sizes. I bought more. Experimented. Failed a lot. Killed Echeverias by overwatering; killed others by not giving them enough light. But I also learned propagation — how to coax new plants from a single fallen leaf. I took photographs. Lots of them. Then I numbered plants with popsicle sticks, organized photo folders, kept spreadsheets.

Things escalated.

By the time the collection moved from a shared office to my own house — where there was actual space and actual sun — I realized I owned far more plants than I’d consciously decided to own. And I was still trying to track them with popsicle sticks and a spreadsheet.

The app I built

The collection outgrew a spreadsheet years before I actually built an app to replace it. Back in 2018, I wanted to track individual plants, attach photographs, log care history — the same things botanical gardens do. I attempted it manually and gave up. In 2026, I finally had the tools to build what I’d always wanted.

It is called Adcock Botanical Garden. Every specimen has an accession number, a location, a photographic timeline and a care history, the way a real botanical garden keeps records.

  • Identification by AI. Point it at a photo and it suggests which of my own plants it is, or what species it might be.
  • Health checks from photographs. It reads a plant against how that species should look, and flags rot, pests or sun damage.
  • Watering, by area. Water a whole yard in one tap, or a single pot.
  • Frost and heat warnings. Wired to the forecast — a cold night produces a list of what needs bringing in, ordered as a walk through the garden.

Built with vanilla HTML, CSS, and JavaScript, backed by Supabase and Google Drive. The hardest part wasn’t the code — it was figuring out how to represent the actual messiness of botanical documentation in a way that felt useful instead of fussy.

Resources I actually use

Books

Succulents and Sunshine taught me the fundamentals when I knew nothing. Debra Lee Baldwin’s books deepened that foundation. Both are worth owning if you want to move past “water it sometimes” into actual understanding.

Nurseries

Ruth Bancroft Garden for interesting and unusual specimens — and their clearance rack, where I’ve found incredible plants at 50% off. Sloat for selection and knowledgeable staff. Lowe’s and Ace for bulk and common plants. Most retailers source from overlapping nurseries anyway, so pick based on what you need: specialty or price.

Identification

Pl@ntNet gives me a starting lead when I’m not sure what something is. From there, I compare images, read botanical descriptions, and sometimes spend an embarrassing amount of time cross-referencing. My own app has become the workflow hub — pointing at a photo, researching the name, storing what I actually own.
